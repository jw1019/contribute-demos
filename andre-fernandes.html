André Fernandes
@andrefernandes.af
Salvador, Bahia, Brazil

I photograph to expand the visibility of and respect for Afro-Brazilian cultures. For over 15 years I have documented the sacred rituals, ornamentation, and spiritual beauty of Candomblé — a practice that connects us to our African ancestors and demands to be seen, understood, and honoured.

My series Orixás, photographed at the Terreiro Ilê Axé Alaketu in Salvador, spent two years in production and was recognised by the United Nations in 2024 as part of the International Art Contest for Minority Artists. It has since been exhibited in Geneva, Asunción, and across Brazil.

How does Candomblé shape your approach to photography?
AF
Candomblé taught me that every object, gesture, and colour carries meaning. When I enter a terreiro, I'm not documenting ceremony — I'm bearing witness to a living theology. The camera becomes a vessel for ancestral memory.
Y
What does the UN recognition mean for Afro-Brazilian art?
AF
It means the world is being asked to look — really look — at what Black Brazilian religiosity holds. Not as folklore. As philosophy, art, and human rights. That visibility is the first step toward respect.
